What would a fully agentic organisation look like? Let us take the thought experiment all the way, without trying to argue with it.

Take an ordinary startup. Everyone there encodes their way of working into skills, designs the agents of their own trade and makes them available to the rest of the company. Legal pours in its contract doctrine, marketing its campaign grammar, finance its rules of engagement, data its business definitions. All that know-how becomes callable by anyone, with no meeting and no queue.

At that point execution is gone. What remains is people with their experience and their nerve, pushing the company toward profitable growth and market share. This is no longer quite science fiction, given that [execution is already no longer the constraint](/en/2026/01/21/execution-is-no-longer-the-constraint/).

Except that one thing remains, exactly one, and it does not encode.

## An agent is irresponsible

The word should be read literally rather than as an accusation. An agent cannot *answer* for what it does, not through negligence, but because there is nobody inside it to hold to account.

The law already says so, more plainly than any philosophical intuition. An AI agent is legally **property** rather than a person, so it does not contract, is not sued in its own name and repairs nothing. California has even closed the escape hatch by barring defendants from arguing that the AI acted on its own.

Responsibility therefore does not evaporate when you delegate, it travels upward. It travels up to the person who configured the agent and decided to let it run, which is what makes the promise of absolution untenable.

## The gap Jonas had already named

That upward travel has a name, and it is older than computing.

In 1979 Hans Jonas published *[The Imperative of Responsibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Jonas)*. His thesis rests on one uncomfortable observation, since modern technology has given our actions a reach that far exceeds our capacity to answer for them. We have produced something that escapes us, and we remain accountable for it all the same.

Jonas was thinking of nuclear power, yet he describes with unsettling precision what we now live at a wholly different timescale. The problem is not the agent's power but **the distance it puts between the act and whoever answers for it**. I write a configuration on Monday, it acts on Tuesday against a system I no longer watch, and the chain of responsibility has not moved an inch.

His imperative translates here without any forcing. Delegate only what you can still answer for.

## The last metre

We can give everyone the ability to delegate their way of working, and it is even desirable, since that is what gives a small team the reach of a large one. But there always comes a moment when someone performs an act nobody can perform in their place, because that act is the one by which they take it on. Merging the code, committing a spend, signing off the business decision drawn from an analysis. A bad decision built on wrong figures remains the data team's responsibility, whatever agent produced the figure.

There is a technical reason for this, and it is sturdier than an argument from principle. [The builder says yes to everything](/en/2026/02/18/the-manual-is-the-product/), since an agent never pushes back and never points out that the request is absurd. A system without a counterweight does not need to be malicious to drift, it only needs to be obedient. The counterweight therefore has to stay human, and [saying no remains the most thankless skill](/en/2026/03/14/data-team-explore-or-disappear/) as much as the most necessary one.

## But responsibilities do fall away

The objection deserves to be taken seriously, because it is already coming true.

Delaware, the American state with the deepest experience in company law, is studying a new kind of entity called an *AIC*, where an autonomous system could run a company, sign contracts and be sued in its own name. This is still only a proposal tested inside a supervised regulatory sandbox, but the direction is set.

Nor is there any need to wait for the law, because the thing is already slipping inside organisations. A continuous integration pipeline whose gates end up merging without review. An advertising budget that reallocates itself. Each time, this is not a delegated responsibility but an **abandoned** one, and out of convenience rather than by decision.

That is the whole difference, and it cuts sharply. Delegating requires deciding and accepting what comes out of it, whereas letting go requires nothing at all. [Power no longer needs you to say yes, it only needs you not to say no](/en/2026/01/03/servitude-by-ergonomics/).

## The org chart says nothing any more

If that reasoning holds, the way we picture companies has become obsolete. An org chart answers the question of who commands whom, and that question assumed work meant distributing execution among people. Once execution goes to agents, the pyramid becomes a drawing that describes nothing.

I have argued here before that [the chain of command loses its purpose once the value chain fits inside one person](/en/2026/05/15/adam-smith-packs-up-his-pins/). But the disappearance of titles leaves an open question nobody is addressing, that of what is then left to describe an organisation with. The answer seems to me the one that still holds, that of **who answers for what**.

At Anthropic everyone carries the same job title, and Boris Cherny describes [five roles](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/building-claude-code-with-boris-cherny) that are precisely not jobs. This is a considerable advance on the classic org chart, but those roles describe ways of working rather than scopes of accountability, and a Prototyper and a Maintainer plainly do not answer for the same things. That second map is the one missing, and it meets a question I left open elsewhere, that of [who holds the mandate to define an agent's behaviour](/en/2026/02/13/who-writes-the-constitution-of-machines/). Configuring without answering for anything is the very definition of power without counterpart.

## What it changes in practice

The exercise does not call for an offsite but for a table, and it consists of going back to job descriptions, not to read tasks off them, which are now largely automatable, but to extract the scopes each person answers for.

| What the agent does | Who answers for it | The last metre |
|---|---|---|
| Writes and proposes code | the maintainer | the merge |
| Produces an analysis | the data team | the decision that invokes it |
| Allocates a media budget | marketing | the spend commitment |
| Replies to a customer | support | what is promised on the company's behalf |
| Merges without review | nobody | none |

The last row is the only test that matters. An empty "who answers for it" column does not signal successful automation but a hole in the structure, and that hole will only surface the day something goes wrong. The exercise has a useful side effect too, since it makes visible that some responsibilities never had a holder, even before agents.

## What we still agree to carry

We will automate nearly everything, and there is nothing to regret in that prospect, because most of what leaves should never have occupied capable people for so long.

But a last metre will always remain, the one where somebody says they will take it on. That metre is not a residue of work that escaped optimisation and that some next generation of models will absorb. It is what makes a company a company rather than a process, and Jonas would likely say it is the last place where our power to act and our capacity to answer for it still coincide.

The question is therefore not what we can delegate, since the answer is now "nearly everything". It is what we still agree to carry.
