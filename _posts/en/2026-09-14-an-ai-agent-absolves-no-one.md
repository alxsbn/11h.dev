What would a fully agentic organisation look like? Let us take the thought experiment all the way, without trying to argue with it.

Take an ordinary startup. Everyone there encodes their way of working into skills, designs the agents of their own trade and makes them available to the others. Legal pours in its contract doctrine, marketing its campaign grammar, data its business definitions, and all that know-how becomes callable by anyone with no meeting and no queue.

At that point execution is gone, and what remains is people with their experience and their nerve. This is no longer quite science fiction, given that [execution is already no longer the constraint](/en/2026/01/21/execution-is-no-longer-the-constraint/).

Except that one thing remains, exactly one, and it does not encode.

## An agent is irresponsible

The word should be read literally rather than as an accusation. An agent cannot *answer* for what it does, not through negligence, but because there is nobody inside it to hold to account.

The law already says so, more plainly than any philosophical intuition. An AI agent is legally **property** rather than a person, so it does not contract, is not sued in its own name and repairs nothing. California has even closed the last escape hatch with [AB 316](https://www.mclellanlawgroup.com/california-ab-316-ai-liability-company-responsibility), in force since January 2026, which bars defendants from arguing that the AI caused the harm on its own. Responsibility therefore does not evaporate when you delegate, it travels up to whoever configured the agent and decided to let it run.

## Our acts reach further than we do

That upward travel has a name older than computing. Hans Jonas, a German philosopher exiled to the United States and a student of Heidegger, published *[The Imperative of Responsibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Jonas)* in 1979. In his account, modern technology has given our actions a reach that far exceeds our capacity to answer for them, so we now produce effects that escape us and for which we remain accountable all the same.

Jonas was thinking of nuclear power, yet he describes what we now live at a wholly different timescale. The problem is not the agent's power but **the distance it puts between the act and whoever answers for it**. I write a configuration on Monday, it acts on Tuesday against a system I no longer watch, and the chain of responsibility has not moved an inch. His imperative translates without forcing, since it amounts to delegating only what you can still answer for.

## The last metre

Delegating your way of working is desirable, since that is what gives a small team the reach of a large one. But there always comes a moment when someone performs an act nobody can perform in their place, the one by which they take it on.

Merging the code, committing a spend, signing off the business decision drawn from an analysis. In my own case, running data, a bad decision built on wrong figures remains my responsibility, whatever agent produced them.

The reason is structural rather than moral. An agent carries out the request it is given, never objecting that it is badly framed or that the result will be unusable, because it has nothing to lose by being wrong. A system does not need to be malicious to drift, it only needs to obey without contesting. Which is why [saying no remains the most thankless skill](/en/2026/03/14/data-team-explore-or-disappear/) as much as the most necessary one, and it is a skill that presupposes having something to lose.

## Responsibilities do fall away

The objection deserves to be taken seriously, because it is already coming true. Delaware is [studying an entity called an *AIC*](https://fortune.com/2026/07/14/exclusive-delaware-ai-agents-legal-entity-proposal-llc-pbc/), an Artificial Intelligence Company, where an autonomous system would run a business with no human at the helm, sign contracts and be sued in its own name. It is still only a thirty-month regulatory sandbox, but the state that hosts most American companies would not have opened it without reason.

Nor is there any need to wait for the law, because the thing is already slipping inside organisations. A continuous integration pipeline whose gates end up merging without review, an advertising budget that reallocates itself. These responsibilities are not delegated but **abandoned**, and the distinction cuts sharply. Delegating is a decision, taken at an identifiable moment by someone who accepts what will come of it, whereas abandoning asks only that you do nothing. [Power no longer needs you to say yes since it only needs you not to say no](/en/2026/01/03/servitude-by-ergonomics/).

## The org chart says nothing any more

If that reasoning holds, the way we picture companies has become obsolete. An org chart answers the question of who commands whom, and that question assumed work meant distributing execution among people. Once execution goes to agents, the pyramid describes nothing.

I have argued here before that [the chain of command loses its purpose once the value chain fits inside one person](/en/2026/05/15/adam-smith-packs-up-his-pins/), but the disappearance of titles leaves open the question of what is then left to describe an organisation with. The answer seems to me the one that still holds, that of **who answers for what**.

Let us run the exercise. We go back to job descriptions, not to read off tasks that are now automatable, but to extract the scopes each person answers for. And there surfaces what [the work-to-rule strike](/en/2026/01/01/the-cheating-that-makes-the-world-run/) already taught us, namely that a job description never lists everything. Following it to the letter is enough to paralyse a company, because most of the real work lives in the zones nobody bothered to write down.

Those unwritten zones were tolerable as long as somebody handled them out of habit. They stop being tolerable the moment an agent moves into one.

Take backups, whose ownership appears in no job description, as is the case in plenty of companies. An agent restores a backup without referring to anyone, and the restore fails. Does the fault lie with whoever designed the agent, with whoever left the line out of the job description, or with the CTO who managed the person who should have answered for it had the role been defined? None of these answers is obvious, and that is exactly the problem. Before agents, this vagueness cost a telling-off and a fix. Now it has no holder at all.

At Anthropic everyone carries the same job title and Boris Cherny describes [five roles](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/building-claude-code-with-boris-cherny) that are precisely not jobs. This is a considerable advance, but those roles describe ways of working rather than scopes of accountability. That second map is the one missing, and it meets a question left open elsewhere, that of [who holds the mandate to define an agent's behaviour](/en/2026/02/13/who-writes-the-constitution-of-machines/). Configuring without answering for anything is the very definition of power without counterpart.

## What we still agree to carry

We will automate nearly everything, and there is nothing to regret, because most of what leaves should never have occupied capable people for so long.

But a last metre will always remain, the one where somebody says they will take it on. That metre is not a residue of work some next generation of models will absorb, it is what makes a company a company rather than a process. Jonas would likely say it is the last place where our power to act and our capacity to answer for it still coincide.

The question is therefore not what we can delegate, since the answer is now "nearly everything". It is what we still agree to carry.
