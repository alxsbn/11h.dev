What would a fully agentic organisation look like? Take an ordinary startup where everyone encodes their way of working into skills and designs the agents of their own trade. Legal pours in its contract doctrine, marketing its campaign grammar, data its definitions, and all that know-how becomes callable by anyone. This is no longer science fiction, and [execution is already no longer the constraint](/en/2026/01/21/execution-is-no-longer-the-constraint/).

One question remains that this organisation cannot hand to its agents, which is who answers for what they do.

## The corporation already answered, halfway

The objection is that an agent can perfectly well answer for its acts, and it is a serious one. Delaware is [studying an entity called an *AIC*](https://fortune.com/2026/07/14/exclusive-delaware-ai-agents-legal-entity-proposal-llc-pbc/), an Artificial Intelligence Company, where an autonomous system would run a business, sign contracts and be sued in its own name. Nothing about this is conceptually new. Since the nineteenth century the joint-stock company has been a legal fiction that contracts, gets convicted and pays damages with nobody inside it.

Yet the corporation never removed the question of who answers. It moved it onto named people, a director, shareholders who put up capital, an auditor who signs the accounts. The AIC follows the same path. To let an agent run a business, the Delaware proposal requires minimum capital, an activity log, disclosure to counterparties and a committee able to suspend the authorisation. Before the agent acts, someone has to write down who pays if it gets things wrong.

That is all I am arguing here. An agent can carry a responsibility, provided someone has decided which one, with what at stake, and who takes it back when the stake is not enough.

## What a refusal costs

Agents in 2026 know how to refuse. They flag an ambiguous request, ask for clarification and decline what looks dangerous. What they cannot do is pay the price of a refusal, which means holding a no against whoever commissioned them for six months while risking something.

That stake is nothing metaphysical. An employment contract, a guarantee or an insurance policy manufactures it, and one could manufacture it for an agent with a deposit or a committed budget. Nobody has done so yet in an ordinary company. Until then, merging the code, committing a spend or signing off a decision drawn from an analysis remain acts someone takes on.

In my own case, running data, a bad decision built on wrong figures remains my responsibility, whatever agent produced them. That is the last metre, the one where somebody says they will take it on.

## A debt the agent calls in

California now bars defendants, through [AB 316](https://www.mclellanlawgroup.com/california-ab-316-ai-liability-company-responsibility), from arguing that the AI caused the harm on its own. The law is narrow. It removes one line of defence without saying who among the designer, the deployer and whoever configured the agent must answer, and that is precisely the question a company faces.

Take backups, whose ownership appears in no job description, as is the case in plenty of companies. An agent restores a backup without referring to anyone, and the restore fails. Does the fault lie with whoever designed the agent, with whoever left the line out of the job description, or with the CTO who managed the person who should have answered for it?

This vagueness was not born with the agent. [The work-to-rule strike](/en/2026/01/01/the-cheating-that-makes-the-world-run/) has always shown that a job description never lists everything, and that most real work lives in what nobody wrote down. While a human handled backups out of habit, this ownerless zone cost at worst a telling-off and a fix. The agent does not open the hole, it makes the hole impossible to fill by habit.

Organisations were already carrying an accountability debt, and agents call it in.

## Draw the map from the agents

Going back to job descriptions will therefore not be enough, since they leave out exactly what matters. The map has to start from the other end, from the agents themselves, and replace an org chart that says who commands whom without ever saying who answers for what.

Every agent gets a named holder at the moment it is deployed. The map is updated each time the agent's scope changes. Any zone left without a holder falls by default to whoever authorised the deployment.

Without that last rule, responsibility is not delegated but abandoned, and [power no longer needs you to say yes when it is enough that you do not say no](/en/2026/01/03/servitude-by-ergonomics/). Delegating is a decision taken by someone who accepts what will come of it. Abandoning asks only that you do nothing.

## What we still agree to carry

We will automate nearly everything, and there is nothing to regret. A last metre will still remain, the one where someone takes things on, and that metre existed long before the agents that now merely present the bill.

The question is therefore not what we can delegate, since the answer is now "nearly everything". It is what we still agree to carry.
